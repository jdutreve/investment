# The ingested books — ten insights each, with their measured record

Owner request, 2026-11-14. The five books in the corpus, and for each the ten
central claims the curator drew from them, read against what measurement says
of them. Figures are those of the live database on 2026-11-14, after that
day's re-measurement of every invariant on the repaired year-on-year series
(`docs/EPISODE_COUNTING.md`); 1,078 claims are cited from the books, one is
`integrated` (Dalio's, item 11 below).

**How to read the tags.**

- **(m: 71/127 = 0.56; null 0.52)**: the claim has a measurable condition and
  was confronted on 127 decided 12-week moments since 1991; it was confirmed
  71 times. The null is the rate its own protocol confirms on every date
  (`invariant.null_confirmation_rate`). Below about twenty moments the rate
  says almost nothing.
- **(note)**: a reference note, with no condition and no effect, so it cannot
  be measured (it carries no weight).

What the measurements say overall: the claims furthest from the null rest on
few moments (0.65 to 0.70 on 19 to 23 moments: a starting real yield and bond
returns, gold far above its trend and bonds, momentum in corrections), then
the equity-trend claims (0.58 to 0.60 on 43 to 61). Negative real rates and
gold fell to 0.53 once re-measured. The cycle claims as the curator wrote them (growth/inflation quadrants,
a WIDENING credit spread, an extreme spread) sit at the null.

That last point is not a verdict on the adopted stack, which reads the credit
spread differently: its LEVEL against its ten-year median, crossed with the
slope, picks one of four books each month, under a trend overlay. An
invariant asks whether one asset class beats the median of the others over the
next twelve weeks; the stack's rule is measured as a whole, in
`docs/V1_STRATEGY.md` and `research_findings.FINDINGS` (2026-11-01): far
ahead of a book picked each January with the data of the time (Sortino +0.415,
1987-2026) and of SPY 60 / GLD 40 held (1.071 against 0.629, drawdown −29%
against −48%), but only +0.125 ahead of the best frozen book known in hindsight
(16% of resamples at or below zero; +0.052 since 1993) — positive, established
on no window.

## Dan Rasmussen — Countercyclical Investing (83 passages)

The book the adopted market-signal stack comes from (ADR-007).

1. Two market signals tell the phase of the cycle: the high-yield spread and
   the 10y − 1y slope, each against its trailing 10-year median. (note — the
   rule built on it is the stack, measured as a whole; see the head of this
   document)
2. Three books: growth (50% trend-followed S&P, 40% small value, 10% gold),
   inflation and slowdown. The measured quadrant claims sit at the null.
   (m: 48/88 = 0.55; null 0.48)
3. A spread above its median → small value earns excess returns; a spread
   doubled (8.7% in March 2020) → the best forward returns on small value and
   high yield. (note; the measured version has a single moment, 0/1)
4. A flat curve with a tight spread anticipates inflation, hence 40% gold
   coming into 2020. (m: 57/104 = 0.55; null 0.51)
5. With the S&P below its 200-day average, bonds beat the other asset classes.
   (m: 26/43 = 0.60; null 0.50)
6. A rally inside a downtrend is not a bottom; bargain hunting costs.
   (m: 36/61 = 0.59; null 0.51)
7. The trend rule applies to gold too: out to Treasuries after five days below
   its 200-day average. (note)
8. The strategy "wins by not losing": it captures 88% of up quarters and 21% of
   down quarters; it lags all-equity when the S&P returns more than 15% a
   year. (note)
9. Backtest 1970–2020: 15.8% a year against 10.0% for 60/40, Sharpe 0.82
   against 0.41; the excess return comes in roughly equal shares from the
   cycle, the dynamic allocation and the trend. (note)
10. Trend following and tail hedging are pro-cyclical: they are hammered in
    reversal rallies. (note)

## Mebane Faber — Global Asset Allocation (159 passages)

1. Inflation above 5% → equities underperform the other classes.
   (m: 6/6 = 1.00; null 0.52 — six moments only)
2. Same inflation → bonds underperform too. Not established. (m: 3/5 = 0.60; null 0.50 — five moments)
3. The real risk of bonds is inflation erosion: real drawdowns beyond 60% in
   the US and the UK, beyond 80% in Japan, Germany, Italy and France. (note)
4. Negative real rates → gold beats the median asset class.
   (m: 34/64 = 0.53; null 0.51)
5. In disinflation gold is the worst asset: +0.37% real a year over 1982–2013
   against +8.61% for US equities. Not confirmed. (m: 43/88 = 0.49; null 0.49)
6. The starting real yield predicts bonds' real return: negative at the start,
   negative after. (m: 13/19 = 0.68; null 0.49)
7. A real rate above 3% → bonds and equities both do well. (m: 4/4 and 3/5 —
   too few moments)
8. Diversified allocations end close together: never the best, never the
   worst; over five years anything can happen. (note)
9. The Permanent Portfolio (a quarter each in stocks, bonds, cash and gold)
   has the lowest volatility and drawdown, and earns less than stocks
   (8.53% against 10.21%). (note)
10. A 60/40 takes about 90% of its risk from equities, hence risk parity — but
    risk parity depends on falling rates. (note; m: 6/13 = 0.46; null 0.44)

## Dimson, Marsh, Staunton — Triumph of the Optimists (1,032 passages)

1. Over long horizons equities beat bonds in all 16 countries of 1900–2000,
   even those devastated by war. (m: 48/97 = 0.49; null 0.48 — at the null)
2. The historical equity premium overstates the forward premium: the post-1950
   one was inflated by factors that will not repeat. (note)
3. The term premium is tiny against its risk: about 0.5% a year across 16
   countries. The measured version (a steep curve → long bonds beat bills) is
   not confirmed. (m: 37/80 = 0.46; null 0.50)
4. Cash barely preserves purchasing power (0.2% to 2% real a year), and its
   best years fall in deflation. (note)
5. Inflation above 5% → equities stay below their usual real return.
   (m: 7/7 — few moments)
6. "In high inflation, equities still beat nominal bonds" is contradicted by
   this history. (m: 0/6)
7. Hyperinflation wipes out every nominal claim: savings, bonds, annuities,
   pensions. (note)
8. A rising real yield → TIPS and long bonds underperform
   (m: 17/28 = 0.61; null 0.50); the starting real yield dominates the forward
   bond return (m: 13/20 = 0.65; null 0.51).
9. A break around 1980: real rates averaged −0.7% over 1900–1979 and +3.7% over
   1980–2000. (note)
10. Value beats growth over a century and across countries, but the premium can
    stay negative for years. (note)

## Ray Dalio — Principles for Navigating Big Debt Crises (1,580 passages)

Ten central claims, and an eleventh because it is the corpus's only integrated one.

1. A debt crisis is manageable when the debt is in the currency the policy
   makers control; foreign-currency debt leads to an inflationary depression.
   (note)
2. The "beautiful deleveraging" balances austerity, defaults, redistribution
   and money printing so that nominal growth exceeds nominal rates. Not
   confirmed. (m: 28/60 = 0.47; null 0.49)
3. Solvency or liquidity: a solvency crisis needs equity, a run needs cash or
   guarantees. (note)
4. Rates at zero and QE → markets bottom shortly after (note); but "QE lowers
   long yields" is rejected. (m: 17/52 = 0.33)
5. Low volatility invites leverage; the bubble is popped by tightening and
   spreads through the levered funding channels. Its measured form — rising
   short rates → equities underperform — is the book's best-measured claim.
   (m: 71/127 = 0.56; null 0.52)
6. An inverted curve → cash beats the median class (m: 7/8 = 0.88; null 0.49 —
   few moments); equities do not clearly underperform (m: 9/17).
7. Widening credit spreads put bonds ahead of equities. Measured: confirmed
   exactly as often as chance would, so no effect in this history.
   (m: 40/81 = 0.49; null 0.50)
8. Negative real rates → gold beats (m: 34/64 = 0.53; null 0.51); when
   debasement is feared (gold far above its trend) government bonds lose their
   haven role (m: 15/23 = 0.65; null 0.50).
9. Uninsured short-term wholesale funding runs at the first sign of trouble;
   guarantees stop the run. (note)
10. Wealth gaps widen in bubbles and populism rises in the downturn; wealth
    taxes raise little. (note)
11. Integrated since the re-measurement of 2026-11-14, the corpus's only one: a
    central bank cutting short rates while the economy is already accelerating
    fuels credit that flows into equities, which beat the median asset class.
    (m: 46/69 = 0.67; null 0.48)

## Cédric Froment — Meilleures stratégies pour gagner sur les actions (386 passages)

1. With the index above its long-term trend, the long side works. A weak link.
   (m: 65/126 = 0.52; null 0.48)
2. With the S&P below its 200-day average, equities underperform
   (m: 35/60 = 0.58; null 0.52), and a long-only investor should stand aside
   (m: 31/52 = 0.60; null 0.49).
3. A trend filter on the index avoids long directionless markets (the Nikkei
   problem). (m: 72/135 = 0.53; null 0.49)
4. A four-phase model classifies the market by price against its 50-day
   average and the 50-day against the 200-day. (note)
5. In a bear market even the best setups fail, and momentum leadership
   collapses. (m: 14/20 = 0.70; null 0.58)
6. The sector explains about half of a stock's move. (note)
7. A breakout counts only with volume rising on the breakout and falling
   during the base. (note)
8. Take a trade only if the target is more than twice the possible loss
   (target from the ATR, stop below the last swing low). (note)
9. Position size follows the phase: 1–1.5% risk per trade on entering phase
   IV, then 0.5–1% after three months. (note)
10. Earnings play: an exceptional surprise on a neglected small float; exit on
    a volume climax; cut at once if the market reacts against the expected
    direction. (note)
